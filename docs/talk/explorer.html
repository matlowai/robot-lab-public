01 · The stack

Nobody trains the whole robot. You train one layer.

A working robot is a stack of controllers, each running faster and closer to the metal than the one above. Our patrol robot walks with NVIDIA's pretrained locomotion policy. The layer we trained, with PPO, is the one that decides how to steer around people.

04 · The algorithm

PPO: improve, but never too much at once.

Proximal Policy Optimization trains on the robot's own recent experience. For each action it compares the new policy's probability with the old one's (the ratio r) and weights it by how much better than expected the action turned out (the advantage A). The clip stops one batch of luck from rewriting the policy. Drag the handle.

05 · The real training runs

Four versions, two hours each, one honest plateau.

These are the actual logs. The first version faced only people walking in straight lines and reached 96% training success. Each later version made the world harder or the robot's memory longer, and success settled between 30% and 41%.

07 · Watch the video, not the table

Both clips passed the success check. Only one succeeded.

A robot arm learning to put an object in a bowl, scored overnight by an automatic check. Pick the real success.

Episode ACHECK: SUCCESS

Fake. This placement spawned the bowl against the robot with the object already under it. The arm bumped the bowl, and the check (object near the bowl's centre, low enough) fired. Every evaluation reused one seed, so this same bad placement “succeeded” for several checkpoints.

Episode BCHECK: SUCCESS

Real. The fine-tuned 7B world-action model grasps the mug, lifts, carries and places it. Our first genuine closed-loop success: 1 of 6 tries, with the jaw opened before handing over.

The overnight run reported 7 successes. All 7 were bugs in the check. The true score was 0, and only reviewing the videos showed it.

Watch the whole run, not the last screen

Same seed, three drivers, the full six-minute patrol. Each lane ticks off its checkpoints; a dot pops when a robot finishes and a red diamond marks a fall. Watch it to the end: the race exposes a flaw in the test, not the robots. The walkers kept a timetable fitted to the slow robot, so the two faster robots passed every encounter before its walker arrived and never faced the test. The only contacts were late walkers reaching the charger after the robots parked.

08 · Across industries

Every industry gets the same three questions.

What will the machine do, what can we break in simulation before it ships, and what will we measure once it's working near people and property? Pick a field.

09 · Build the capability

It isn't mature yet. That's the reason to start.

The organizations that will be ready are the ones with people who already know how to test these systems. Our proposal is a small core team that builds simulation, evaluation and training skills once and lends them to every business unit and partner.

out: recipes, harness, scorecardsback: scenarios, incidents, failure videos

The core owns

  • simulator setup and scenario templates
  • the evaluation harness: controls, held-out seeds, video review
  • a metrics library normalized by exposure
  • training recipes and shared compute
  • a protocol for testing a vendor's system

Each unit owns

  • its scenarios and edge cases
  • site maps and real incident data
  • acceptance thresholds
  • feedback from operations

The tools are open

Isaac Lab, LeRobot, and open world-action models like FLUX 3 Action all run on one workstation.

Training is cheap

2 billion steps took 2 hours. Knowing whether the result was real took days.

Vendors arrive first

You'll be asked to approve, buy or answer for systems you didn't build.

Skills transfer

Agent and LLM teams already have half of it: rollouts, evals, reward design.

Weeks 1–2

One workstation

Install a simulator, train a toy task, run a pretrained robot.

Weeks 3–4

One real scenario

Rebuild a situation from your own operations, with a scripted baseline.

Weeks 5–8

Evaluation first

Controls, held-out seeds, video review, per-exposure metrics. Before any training.

Weeks 9–12

Train, score, share

Train a policy or test a vendor's. Publish the full table. Hand the kit to a second team.
